# Constitutional Review — 2026-08-25

**Outcome: all 14 articles, the Preamble and Amendment I bind as written. Zero amendments.
The constitution is unchanged at 188 of its 190-line budget.**

That is the finding, not an absence of one. Between ratification (2026-06-10) and this review the
regime absorbed a frontier-model generation change, a vendor removed for 19 days by an
export-control directive, a ~33% cut in available compute, three session topologies that did not
exist when it was written (migration, forking, peer agents), and a nine-round internal dispute over
one of its own instruments — **without a line needing to change.** Every gap the review found was
one layer down, in enforcement, which is exactly where the Sorting Rule says gaps belong.

## Why the review happened

Art. 11 names a **frontier-model generation change** as a standing trigger: a new model family
becoming the working default convenes a review, whether or not anything looks wrong. It fired. The
review is not an audit of incidents — it asks of each article only: *does this still bind correctly
under current conditions?*

## How it was run

Article by article, with evidence loaded per row, ruled one at a time rather than as a batch. A
partial session three weeks' worth of work earlier had ruled three articles and deferred the rest;
this session completed the deferred set.

**Ruling one at a time was not ceremony.** The partial session's ruled set and deferred set,
added together, accounted for **13 of 14 articles**. The missing one was **Art. 11 — the article
governing amendments itself.** It had fallen between two lists and would have been ratified by
omission. A batch ratification would have inherited the same 13 and never counted.

> **Transferable defence, if you run something like this:** assert that
> `ruled ∪ deferred == the full set`, mechanically. One line of arithmetic. Nothing in our
> tooling did this, which is why it took a human choosing the slower path to catch it.

## What the evidence actually showed

Three findings are worth more than the ruling itself, because they are about how governance text
behaves under pressure rather than about our particular text.

**1. An article bound a document written under every incentive to ignore it.**
Facing a real capacity cut, the budget policy downgraded model tiers across the board — then
stopped at one row and cited the constitution as the reason it could not touch it: *irreversible
operations are never downgraded for cost.* A rule that survives being expensive is a rule that
works.

**2. The constitution was right earlier than its readers were.**
Its diagnostic article says a recurring failure that changes its observable signature invalidates
the previous diagnosis, and that you stop after three fix-verify cycles. On a separate problem we
ran four cycles, each chasing a differently-shaped symptom of one cause. The rule was written,
published, and unheeded — by the people who wrote it. **Recorded as: the article did not fail, its
readers did.** That is enforcement-layer evidence, not an argument for new prose.

**3. Two of our own artifacts made claims their evidence did not support** — during the same
session, on the same day, about work in progress. Both were caught by adversarial review. Neither
was caught by a mechanism. The article forbidding it is correct; nothing was enforcing it.

## What changed as a result

Nothing in the constitution. The follow-through is entirely in the layers below it:

- a **staleness linter** whose assertions are each traced to a specific real incident, rather than
  written from imagination;
- promoting an advisory verification gate to one that can actually **refuse**;
- closing the gap between a routing policy that says binding happens *against live inventory* and
  an implementation that takes its inventory as an argument;
- **arming the governance directory itself**, which had been excluded from its own enforcement
  layer since the tooling was written — the article requiring the enforcement layer to audit itself
  was, at that one point, a false claim.

## For anyone adopting this

**Nothing breaks. v1.0 is still current** — no migration, no re-adoption.

Worth re-pulling: the specs and the adapter kit in this repository have moved since the last
extraction (`spec/NEOCORTEX_SPEC.md` is two ratified revisions newer than what was published
before). Three self-checks ship here and are worth running against your own copy:

| Tool | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| `adapters/claude-code/install_adapters.py --check` | Has any project's adapter drifted from the canonical hook set? |
| `adapters/claude-code/test_hooks.sh` | Do the hooks still fire — and still stay silent — on 23 fire/no-fire cases? |
| `adapters/claude-code/verify_fires.py` | Has a hook **actually fired** in this project, or is it merely installed? |

That last distinction did real work here. A project can be armed for months and never fire; until
you have evidence of firing, an installed hook is a plan, not a control.

## Four lessons that generalise

1. **A test that recomputes its expected value in its own body certifies nothing.** Two of ours
   did. They passed whether or not the fix they guarded was present.
2. **An acceptance check must be specific to the mechanism it certifies.** "Exits non-zero" is
   worthless if the environment produces that signature for free.
3. **Guard-clause ordering can make a failure state unreachable.** One of ours rendered a crashed
   backup as a green status line for two weeks — and the masking condition was *anti-correlated*
   with risk: it was loudest exactly when things were worst.
4. **Ratification by omission is real.** Enumerate, then assert.
